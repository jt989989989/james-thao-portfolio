Reading happens on this device — the camera image is never uploaded. Tap to focus: turn it on, then tap the question on the camera (finger or mouse) to read only that area, then tap the choices to read only those — the two reads are combined and solved, with the answer highlighted on the camera as usual. Only the text inside the green frame is read in a normal scan: fit each question with all of its A–D choices inside it (move back a little rather than in close, so the choices fit too). Why did a scan used to pick up other text? Everything inside the frame is photographed — including papers on the desk beside the phone — and the reader returns all of it; the scanner now rebuilds the read from the words' positions and keeps only the lines that line up with the question's own column, so print beside the quiz no longer lands in the text box or the question. And a read is no longer called finished on the question alone: if the question read but its choices didn't, the scan keeps going — screen passes first, then a pass that crops to just the choice rows and reads those enlarged on their own — before it settles. Pointing at another screen? If the first read comes back unclear, the scan automatically re-reads the frame with a screen-cleanup pass that flattens glow and screen texture — hold steady and fill the frame. Every answer below is one of that question's own printed choices. Questions asked in words are solved too ("What is twelve divided by three?", "Mia shares 24 stickers equally among 4 friends") — and a story problem is answered even when its choices aren't in the frame ("A factory packs 480 juice boxes into packs of 24", "8 shelves, each holds 45 books"), with the worked answer shown right on the camera. The card highlights the question it read, and for word questions it marks the words it turned into a calculation and shows what it read them as. And because a ÷ can scan as a + when its dots are lost in screen glow, any + in a maths question is double-checked against the picture itself before it is trusted. The scanner is taught two anchors, the same way: a question begins at its printed number followed by a period ("2.") — and still counts when the scan loses that thin full stop ("2 Which…") — and its choices begin at the lettered lines that follow — A. B. C. D. — each choice keeping the letter printed beside it, in whatever order those lines arrive. The answers may shuffle around between attempts — the same answer can sit under a different letter on a retake — so the scanner never answers by letter or by position: it works out the answer's text (Ho-Oh, 36, Paris) from the question, then reports whatever letter that text is printed under in the scan in front of it, and highlights that choice where it actually sits. So the question is only the lines between its number and the first choice line, the choices are the lettered run after it, and everything else in the frame is left out: the quiz title and the "4/5" progress marker above the number, the hint text and the Back/Next buttons below the last choice, and any other print that happens to share the frame is never mistaken for the question or its choices. The question number itself is never treated as part of the maths. If a question is read but its choices aren't in the frame, you still get a suggested answer worked from the question alone, labelled as a suggestion. And if the question itself comes back garbled — words misread so it doesn't quite make sense — the scanner fills in the blanks: each word it doesn't recognise is swapped for the closest word it does know (from the question's own choices and its built-in banks), the card shows the filled-in question it used, and the solvers try again on that. If an answer still can't be confirmed after all that, the scanner offers its best guess as a SUGGESTED answer instead of nothing: on the camera it appears in the answer banner with an amber chip and the suggested choice is boxed in amber dashes (never the solid green of a confirmed answer), and on the card it is tagged SUGGESTED ANSWER — BEST GUESS, NOT CONFIRMED. And if the worked answer doesn't match any of that question's choices, the symbols and digits are re-analysed against the choices before anything is reported — a ÷ that read as + and a ? that read as an extra 7 are both retried the way the choices support (432 + 127 is re-read as 432 ÷ 12 when 36 is one of the choices). If the question and its choices still don't agree, the scan re-reads the frame with different treatments — a hard black-and-white pass, then flipped colours — and keeps whichever read makes them agree. So the answer the choices point to is the one you see, highlighted on the camera with the working that picked it shown on the highlighted choice. Every choice the scanner can see is outlined in blue on the camera as soon as it is found — solved or not — and a CHOICES SEEN pill at the top of the camera lists the letters it found, so you can tell it has located the multiple-choice section before it answers; the correct choice then gets the solid green box. On the live camera each scan first grabs a few frames in a burst and reads the sharpest one, because a shaky grab smears the letters — hold the phone steady (or brace it) and more questions read correctly the first time. And if a question and its choices still don't agree after every re-read, the scanner grabs a fresh frame and scans the question again before settling. If a read still gets the question or its choices wrong, you can teach it: in the text box below, select the lines that really are the question — or the lines that really are the choices — and tap the matching Mark button; it re-solves from your correction and keeps the answer highlighted on the camera. And when a question with choices can't be solved from what's on the phone (maths, the built-in fact and security banks, definitions), the scanner looks it up on Wikipedia — free, no account — checks each printed choice against what it finds, and labels that card "ANSWER FROM A WEB LOOKUP" so it's clear the answer was looked up, not calculated; maths is always worked out on the phone itself.
